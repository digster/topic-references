Data Structures and Algorithms

Last updated: 2026-07-28

Data structures are the standard ways of organizing data in memory — arrays, linked lists, hash tables, trees, heaps, graphs — and algorithms are the standard procedures for computing over them: sorting, searching, traversing, and optimizing. The two are studied together because the choice of structure determines which algorithms are even possible, and asymptotic analysis (Big-O notation) is the shared language for saying how the cost of a choice grows as the input gets bigger.

This is the most transferable subject in computer science: it is what separates a program that works on a hundred items from one that still works on a hundred million. It underpins every database index, compiler, router and search engine you have ever used, it is the substance of most technical interviews, and it is the vocabulary engineers reach for when arguing about why something is slow.

Good to know first: comfort writing loops and functions in at least one programming language, familiarity with arrays and lists, and light math — exponents and logarithms are enough to read a Big-O bound. No advanced mathematics is needed to start.

📚 Books

  • Grokking Algorithms (2nd ed.) — Aditya Y. Bhargava, Manning (2024) Beginner

    Over 400 hand-drawn illustrations make the classic algorithms click; the friendliest possible on-ramp.

  • Open Data Structures — Pat Morin, Carleton University Beginner

    Free and Creative Commons–licensed, with the same text available in Java, C++, Python and pseudocode editions.

  • The Algorithm Design Manual (3rd ed.) — Steven S. Skiena, Springer (2020) Intermediate

    The practitioner's book: half design techniques, half a catalog of 75 problems that actually come up, with war stories throughout.

  • Algorithms — Jeff Erickson, University of Illinois (2019) Intermediate

    A free, full-color textbook that is unusually good on recursion, backtracking and dynamic programming — and genuinely fun to read.

  • Introduction to Algorithms (4th ed.) — Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest & Stein, MIT Press (2022) Advanced

    "CLRS" — the standard reference. Dense and proof-heavy; best used to look things up rather than read cover to cover.

📄 Papers & Primary References

  • Self-Adjusting Binary Search Trees — Daniel Sleator & Robert Tarjan, JACM (1985) Advanced

    Introduces the splay tree and, more importantly, shows what amortized analysis buys you — a structure with no balance information that is still O(log n) per operation.

  • Engineering a Sort Function — Jon Bentley & Douglas McIlroy (1993) Advanced

    The gap between a textbook quicksort and a production one, written by the people who closed it. The best case study in algorithm engineering there is.

✍️ Articles & Blog Posts

  • Big-O Cheat Sheet — Eric Drowell Beginner

    One-page time and space complexity tables for every common data structure and sorting algorithm — the reference you'll keep open.

  • basecs — Vaidehi Joshi Beginner

    A year-long series explaining one CS fundamental a week, in plain language with drawings. Excellent for filling gaps if you skipped a CS degree.

  • Nearly All Binary Searches and Mergesorts Are Broken — Joshua Bloch, Google Research (2006) Intermediate

    An overflow bug that survived decades in Programming Pearls and the Java standard library — a short, humbling lesson in why "obviously correct" isn't.

🎥 Videos & Talks

  • Algorithms (playlist) — Abdul Bari Beginner

    Patient whiteboard walkthroughs of each algorithm, traced by hand on real inputs. The go-to when a written explanation isn't landing.

  • 6.006 Introduction to Algorithms — Lecture Videos — Erik Demaine, Jason Ku & Justin Solomon, MIT OCW (Spring 2020) Intermediate

    21 lectures of a real MIT course, free and complete — rigorous but taught with unusual clarity about why each structure exists.

🎓 Courses & Tutorials

  • Algorithms, Part I — Robert Sedgewick & Kevin Wayne, Princeton (Coursera) Beginner

    Union-find, sorting, searching, BSTs and hash tables, with famously well-designed programming assignments. Free in full.

  • Algorithms, Part II — Robert Sedgewick & Kevin Wayne, Princeton (Coursera) Intermediate

    The graph and string half — shortest paths, spanning trees, max-flow, tries, substring search and compression. Also free.

  • Algorithms, 4th Edition booksite (algs4) — Robert Sedgewick & Kevin Wayne, Princeton Intermediate

    The companion site to the two courses above: complete Java implementations, exercises with solutions, and excerpts. Useful standalone.

  • 6.006 Introduction to Algorithms (full course) — MIT OpenCourseWare Intermediate

    Everything behind the lectures — notes, recitations, problem sets, exams and solutions — if you want to actually do the course rather than watch it.

  • Algorithms Illuminated — Tim Roughgarden, Stanford / Columbia Intermediate

    A four-part book series whose lecture videos and slides are free on the site — the accessible route to Roughgarden's Stanford course, whose Coursera version is paid.

🔧 Tools & Interactive

  • VisuAlgo — Steven Halim, National University of Singapore

    24 animated modules covering everything from sorting to suffix arrays and max-flow, and you can feed in your own inputs.

  • Data Structure Visualizations — David Galles, University of San Francisco

    Step-by-step animations you drive one operation at a time — the clearest way to finally understand AVL and red-black tree rotations.

  • CSES Problem Set — Antti Laaksonen, University of Helsinki

    400+ problems grouped by technique rather than difficulty, so you can drill one topic at a time instead of grinding random puzzles.

🔗 Further Reading / Going Deeper

  • Competitive Programmer's Handbook — Antti Laaksonen (free PDF) Intermediate

    The companion to the CSES problem set, and the most efficient summary anywhere of the techniques contests actually test.

  • CP-Algorithms — community-maintained Advanced

    Encyclopedic write-ups with working code for algorithms most textbooks skip — segment trees, heavy-light decomposition, suffix automata.

  • 6.851 Advanced Data Structures — Erik Demaine, MIT OCW Advanced

    The research frontier: persistence, retroactivity, cache-oblivious structures and succinct encodings. Where to go after CLRS stops.

  • Purely Functional Data Structures — Chris Okasaki, CMU thesis (1996) Advanced

    How to build efficient structures when nothing can be mutated — essential if you work in a functional language or care about persistence.

  • The Art of Computer Programming — Donald Knuth, Stanford Advanced

    The definitive multi-volume treatment, still being written after 60 years. Volume 3 on sorting and searching is the one to reach for first.
