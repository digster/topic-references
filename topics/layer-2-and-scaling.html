Layer 2 and Scaling

Last updated: 2026-08-01

A blockchain is slow on purpose: every node re-executes every transaction, so throughput is capped by the weakest machine you want to be able to verify the chain. A layer 2 breaks that trade-off by moving execution off the main chain while still posting enough data back to it that anyone can reconstruct and challenge the result. The dominant design is the rollup, in two flavours — optimistic rollups assume batches are valid and allow a fraud proof during a challenge window, ZK rollups post a validity proof so there is nothing to challenge.

This is where Ethereum's roadmap actually went: since 2020 the base layer has been optimised to be a settlement and data-availability layer for rollups rather than an execution layer for users, with EIP-4844's blobs cutting L2 costs by orders of magnitude. It matters because it determines where applications get deployed, what a transaction costs, and — through sequencers, upgrade keys and escape hatches — how much of the security story is real.

Good to know first: how Ethereum executes transactions and what gas pays for. The ZK rollup material is much easier after a first pass at zero-knowledge proofs, but you can treat validity proofs as a black box on a first read.

📄 Papers & Primary References

  • A rollup-centric ethereum roadmap — Vitalik Buterin, Ethereum Magicians (2020) Intermediate

    The forum post that redirected the entire protocol roadmap away from execution sharding. Short, and everything since follows from it.

  • EIP-4844: Shard Blob Transactions — Buterin, Feist, Loerakker, Kadianakis, Garnett, Taiwo & Dietrichs (2022) Advanced

    "Proto-danksharding": a transaction type carrying data that's cheap, verifiable and deliberately deleted after ~18 days. The change that made L2 fees negligible.

  • Scaling — Ethereum developer docs — ethereum.org Beginner

    The maintained map of every approach and how they compare, with dedicated pages on optimistic rollups and ZK rollups. Start here.

  • Data Availability — ethereum.org Advanced

    The problem that decides whether a rollup is a rollup: proving data was actually published without downloading it. Covers sampling and the committee designs.

  • Danksharding — ethereum.org Advanced

    Where the blob design is heading — many more blobs per block, secured by data-availability sampling rather than by every node downloading everything.

  • A scalable verification solution for blockchains — Jason Teutsch & Christian Reitwießner (2019) Advanced

    The TrueBit paper, where interactive dispute resolution — bisecting a disagreement down to a single step — was worked out. Every optimistic rollup's fraud proof is a descendant.

✍️ Articles & Blog Posts

  • Layer 2 — ethereum.org Beginner

    The user-facing version: what an L2 is, what bridging means, and what you're actually trusting when you move funds onto one.

  • An Incomplete Guide to Rollups — Vitalik Buterin (2021) Intermediate

    The canonical explainer. Derives rollups from first principles, works through the compression tricks with real gas numbers, and states the open problems honestly.

  • Rollups — The Ultimate Ethereum Scaling Solution — Finematics Beginner

    The friendlier walkthrough of the same material, with diagrams. Good to read before Vitalik's piece if the gas arithmetic there is heavy going.

  • The Limits to Blockchain Scalability — Vitalik Buterin (2021) Intermediate

    Why "just raise the block size" fails — bandwidth, storage growth and the number of people who can still run a node. The constraint every design on this page is working around.

  • Endgame — Vitalik Buterin (2021) Advanced

    The argument that block production centralises regardless of the design, so the goal should be keeping *validation* cheap and censorship-resistant. Shapes how to read every L2's roadmap.

  • What kind of layer 3s make sense? — Vitalik Buterin (2022) Advanced

    A sceptical look at stacking rollups on rollups — which layer-3 designs add something and which are just marketing. Useful immunisation against the appchain pitch.

🔧 Tools & Interactive

  • L2BEAT — L2BEAT Intermediate

    Independent analytics on every L2, and far more useful than the TVL numbers: the risk framework grades each one on proof systems, sequencer failure and upgrade keys. The reality check on marketing claims.

  • growthepie — growthepie Beginner

    Open dashboards on L2 usage, fees and blob consumption over time. The quickest way to see what actually happened to costs after EIP-4844.

  • Arbitrum Documentation — Offchain Labs Advanced

    The most thoroughly documented optimistic rollup — read the Nitro sections on interactive fraud proofs and the WASM-based dispute game.

  • Optimism Documentation — Optimism Advanced

    The OP Stack, documented as a reusable rollup framework rather than one chain. The clearest picture of what building an L2 from parts now involves.

  • zkSync Documentation — Matter Labs Advanced

    A production ZK rollup with an EVM-compatible zkVM — useful for seeing where validity proofs force the execution environment to differ from mainnet.

  • Starknet Documentation — StarkWare Advanced

    The other end of the design space: a purpose-built language and STARK prover instead of EVM compatibility. The contrast with zkSync is the lesson.

  • Celestia Documentation — Celestia Advanced

    Data availability as a standalone layer, with sampling implemented in production. The reference for what "modular blockchain" means concretely.
