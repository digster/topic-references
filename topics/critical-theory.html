Critical Theory

Last updated: 2026-09-27

Critical theory, in its original sense, is the tradition of social philosophy that grew out of the Institute for Social Research, founded in Frankfurt in 1923 — Max Horkheimer, Theodor W. Adorno, Herbert Marcuse, Walter Benjamin and the rest of what became known as the Frankfurt School. Horkheimer defined it in 1937 against "traditional" theory: instead of describing society from a neutral distance, a critical theory asks how present arrangements dominate the people living under them, and it aims at their emancipation. Driven into exile in America by the Nazis, the group spent the following decades trying to explain why the revolution Marx predicted never came, and why modern societies produced fascism, a mass-produced culture industry and a comfortable conformism instead.

Jürgen Habermas, the leading figure of the second generation, who died in March 2026, rebuilt the project around communication — the public sphere, and the idea that a norm is legitimate only if everyone affected could accept it in open argument — and spent the 1980s arguing with Foucault and Derrida over whether reason can still ground critique. A third generation, around Axel Honneth and Nancy Fraser, recast it in terms of recognition and justice. In a broader sense the name now covers any theory that criticises society in order to change it — feminist, critical race, postcolonial and queer theory among them — which is why "critical theory" turns up in philosophy, law and literature departments alike, and in political arguments where it is often used far more loosely.

Good to know first: some Marx — alienation, commodity fetishism, ideology — and a little Hegel and Freud, because the first generation read everything through all three; the introductions below supply enough of each to keep going. Almost everything here is translated from German, and translation matters, so the editions are named where it does.

📚 Books

  • Critical Theory: A Very Short Introduction (2nd ed.) — Stephen Eric Bronner, Oxford University Press (2017) Beginner

    The best place to start: organised around the tradition's ideas — alienation and reification, the culture industry, repressive tolerance, non-identity and utopia — rather than a roll-call of names. The second edition adds the resurgence of right-wing and neo-fascist politics. Paid, and cheap.

  • Grand Hotel Abyss: The Lives of the Frankfurt School — Stuart Jeffries, Verso (2016) Beginner

    A lively group biography: who these people were, how they lived in exile, and why their ideas about consumerism and mass culture still bite. The title is Lukács's jibe that Adorno and friends had checked into a comfortable hotel on the edge of the abyss — his 1962 preface, free. Paid.

  • The Dialectical Imagination: A History of the Frankfurt School and the Institute of Social Research, 1923–1950 — Martin Jay, University of California Press (1973; 1996 ed.) Intermediate

    The standard history of the first generation, from the Institute's founding through exile in New York to its return to Frankfurt; the 1996 edition adds a new preface on why the work still matters. Paid. The Institute still exists, and its own history page takes the story to today.

  • Dialectic of Enlightenment: Philosophical Fragments — Max Horkheimer & Theodor W. Adorno, trans. Edmund Jephcott, Stanford University Press (2002; German 1947) Advanced

    The school's central book, written in American exile during the war: the Enlightenment's drive to master nature turns into the domination of people, from Homer's Odysseus to the Hollywood studio. Jephcott's is the translation to use. Paid; an excerpt from the first chapter is free on the publisher's site.

  • Minima Moralia: Reflections from Damaged Life — Theodor W. Adorno, trans. E. F. N. Jephcott, Verso (1974; German 1951) Intermediate

    Adorno at his most readable: 153 short aphorisms written in exile, each turning something ordinary — a home, a gift, a love affair, a slammed door — into a small piece of social critique. "Wrong life cannot be lived rightly" comes from here. Paid.

  • The Authoritarian Personality — Theodor W. Adorno, Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel J. Levinson & R. Nevitt Sanford, Verso (2019; first 1950) Advanced

    The school's great empirical project: surveys and interviews with Americans, scored on an "F scale" for fascist potential — conventionalism, anti-intellectualism, power and toughness, projectivity. Over a thousand pages; this edition's introduction by Peter E. Gordon is the way in. Paid.

  • One-Dimensional Man (2nd ed.) — Herbert Marcuse, introduction by Douglas Kellner, Beacon Press (1991; first 1964) Intermediate

    The book that made Marcuse the philosopher of the 1960s New Left: an affluent society that satisfies the needs it creates leaves no room to imagine anything else — "a comfortable, smooth, reasonable, democratic unfreedom". Paid.

  • The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere: An Inquiry into a Category of Bourgeois Society — Jürgen Habermas, trans. Thomas Burger with Frederick Lawrence, MIT Press (1989; German 1962) Intermediate

    How coffee houses, salons and newspapers created a public that argued about power, and how mass media and advertising hollowed it out again. The starting point for every later argument about the public sphere, including today's about social media. Paid.

  • The Struggle for Recognition: The Moral Grammar of Social Conflicts — Axel Honneth, trans. Joel Anderson, Polity (1995; German 1992) Advanced

    The founding text of the third generation: social conflicts read as struggles for recognition — in love, in rights and in social esteem — built from the young Hegel and G. H. Mead. Paid.

  • Redistribution or Recognition? A Political–Philosophical Exchange — Nancy Fraser & Axel Honneth, trans. Joel Golb, James Ingram & Christiane Wilke, Verso (2003) Advanced

    The third generation's defining debate, staged as a book: Fraser argues that justice needs both redistribution and recognition, Honneth that recognition covers both. Paid; Fraser's side is free in her 1996 Tanner Lectures.

  • Alienation — Rahel Jaeggi, ed. Frederick Neuhouser, trans. Frederick Neuhouser & Alan E. Smith, Columbia University Press (2014; German 2005) Advanced

    The present generation rescuing the tradition's oldest concept: alienation reworked as a failed relation to yourself and the world, without Marx's appeal to a fixed human essence. Paid.

  • The Idea of a Critical Theory: Habermas and the Frankfurt School — Raymond Geuss, Cambridge University Press (1981) Advanced

    A short, rigorous reconstruction, in plain Anglo-American prose, of what "critical" actually claims — above all what ideology critique is and how it could ever be justified. Read it last, to test what you've learned. Paid.

📄 Papers & Primary References

  • Letters from the Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher: Marx to Ruge, September 1843 — Karl Marx (1843), Marxists Internet Archive Beginner

    A short letter the whole tradition keeps quoting: "ruthless criticism of all that exists", and critical philosophy as the age's "self-clarification" of its own struggles — the definition Nancy Fraser starts from below. Free; pair it with the Theses on Feuerbach.

  • Reification and the Consciousness of the Proletariat — Georg Lukács, History and Class Consciousness (1923), Marxists Internet Archive Advanced

    The precursor everyone in Frankfurt had read: the commodity form spreading from the market into how people think, until social relations look like natural facts. Long and hard, but "reification" starts here. Free; the SEP entry on Lukács is the handrail.

  • The Present Situation of Social Philosophy and the Tasks of an Institute for Social Research — Max Horkheimer (1931), trans. John Torpey, Marxists Internet Archive Intermediate

    Horkheimer's inaugural lecture as director, and the Institute's programme: philosophy and the social sciences working together on how economy, psyche and culture hang together. Free. The essay that named the tradition, "Traditional and Critical Theory" (1937), is in his Critical Theory: Selected Essays and has no legitimate free copy online; the SEP entry on Horkheimer summarises it.

  • The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction — Walter Benjamin (1936), trans. Harry Zohn, Marxists Internet Archive Intermediate

    Photography and film strip the artwork of its "aura" of uniqueness — a loss Benjamin half welcomed, because mass art could be politicised. Probably the most-read essay the circle produced, and the start of a long argument with Adorno. Free.

  • On the Concept of History ("Theses on the Philosophy of History") — Walter Benjamin (1940), trans. Dennis Redmond, Marxists Internet Archive Intermediate

    Benjamin's last text, written months before his death while fleeing the Nazis: history seen from the side of the defeated, and the angel of history blown backwards into the future by the storm called progress. Short, and free in a Creative Commons translation.

  • The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception — Max Horkheimer & Theodor W. Adorno (1944), Marxists Internet Archive Intermediate

    The most influential chapter of Dialectic of Enlightenment, most of it free here: film, radio and popular music as an industry that manufactures sameness and sells it as choice. Every later critique of mass media argues with this.

  • Repressive Tolerance — Herbert Marcuse (1965), the official Herbert Marcuse website Intermediate

    Marcuse's most controversial essay: tolerance extended equally to everything, in an unequal society, ends up protecting the powerful — so a liberating tolerance would have to take sides. Still quoted on every side of free-speech arguments; read the text before the summaries. Free.

  • Public Space and Political Public Sphere — The Biographical Roots of Two Motifs in My Thought — Jürgen Habermas, Kyoto Prize commemorative lecture (2004) Beginner

    Habermas explaining himself: how surgery as a newborn, a childhood in the Third Reich and the break of 1945 turned him towards communication and the public sphere. The gentlest way into his work, and free — the English text follows the Japanese, from page 13 of the PDF.

  • The Public Sphere: An Encyclopedia Article (1964) — Jürgen Habermas, trans. Sara Lennox & Frank Lennox, New German Critique 3 (1974) Intermediate

    The argument of Structural Transformation, above, in seven pages. Paywalled on JSTOR.

  • Modernity versus Postmodernity — Jürgen Habermas, trans. Seyla Ben-Habib, New German Critique 22 (1981) Advanced

    His 1980 Adorno Prize speech, better known as "Modernity — An Incomplete Project": the opening shot of his quarrel with French post-structuralism, in which he files the line from Bataille through Foucault to Derrida under "young conservatives". Paywalled on JSTOR.

  • Rethinking the Public Sphere: A Contribution to the Critique of Actually Existing Democracy — Nancy Fraser, Social Text 25/26 (1990) Intermediate

    The classic critique of Habermas's public sphere from inside the tradition: it excluded women and workers from the start, and subordinated groups build "subaltern counterpublics" of their own. Paywalled on JSTOR.

✍️ Articles & Blog Posts

  • Critical Theory (Frankfurt School) — Robin Celikates & Jeffrey Flynn, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (2023) Intermediate

    The best free overview, and a recent one: it separates the narrow sense (the Frankfurt School's generations) from the broad one, and takes the story from Horkheimer's programme to present-day debates.

  • Frankfurt School and Critical Theory — Claudio Corradetti, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy Beginner

    Shorter than the Stanford entry and built around three problems — ideology critique (with a summary of Horkheimer's 1937 essay), theory and practice, and reason. Read it first if the Stanford one is heavy going.

  • The Naysayers — Alex Ross, The New Yorker (2014) Beginner

    The best magazine introduction: Benjamin and Adorno's argument over art in the age of mass media, told by the magazine's music critic and carried through to pop culture now. Metered paywall.

  • Theodor W. Adorno — Henry Pickford & Lambert Zuidervaart, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy Advanced

    The tradition's most demanding thinker laid out: negative dialectics, the critique of the culture industry and the aesthetics, with Adorno's difficulty explained rather than reproduced.

  • Walter Benjamin — Peter Osborne & Matthew Charles, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy Intermediate

    Benjamin was never quite a member of the school, and this entry shows why: the Jewish messianism, the unfinished Arcades Project and the friendship-cum-argument with Adorno.

  • Herbert Marcuse — Arnold Farr, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy Intermediate

    From Heidegger's student to the New Left's philosopher: Marcuse's fusion of Marx and Freud in Eros and Civilization, and the case against one-dimensional society.

  • Jürgen Habermas — James Gordon Finlayson & Dafydd Huw Rees, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy Advanced

    The whole career in one place — the public sphere, communicative action, discourse ethics, law and democracy, and the public interventions. Read it before The Theory of Communicative Action, his two-volume main work.

  • Recognition — Mattias Iser, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy Advanced

    The third generation's central concept mapped: Honneth's three forms of recognition, Fraser's objections, and the Hegelian roots of both.

🎥 Videos & Talks

  • The Frankfurt School: From a Failed Revolution to Critical Theory — Tom Nicholas Beginner

    Under half an hour, and the clearest start: why Marxist intellectuals turned from economics to culture when the revolution failed to arrive, and what Horkheimer meant by critical theory.

  • Walter Benjamin: The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction — Then & Now Beginner

    A patient walk through Benjamin's essay above — aura, reproduction and the politics of film — to watch before you read it.

  • Adorno Lectures 2019: Materialism (1 of 3) — Peter E. Gordon, Institut für Sozialforschung Advanced

    Harvard's Peter Gordon lecturing in Frankfurt for the fiftieth anniversary of Adorno's death, on the Institute's own channel: is Adorno's critique really as bleak as it sounds? The other two lectures are on the same channel, and the series became the book A Precarious Happiness (2023).

🎓 Courses & Tutorials

  • ENGL 300, Lecture 17: The Frankfurt School of Critical Theory — Paul H. Fry, Open Yale Courses Intermediate

    A free filmed lecture with transcript on Adorno and Benjamin in their political context. The same course then follows the heirs: lectures 20 (feminism), 21 (African-American criticism), 22 (post-colonial criticism) and 23 (queer theory).

  • Philosophize This! #108–#112: The Frankfurt School — Stephen West Beginner

    Five free podcast episodes in order: the introduction, the Enlightenment, the culture industry, then two on Marcuse's Eros and Civilization. Episode #143 picks up Habermas and the public sphere.

🔗 Further Reading / Going Deeper

  • What's Critical about Critical Theory? The Case of Habermas and Gender — Nancy Fraser, New German Critique 35 (1985) Advanced

    Where the name starts to travel: Fraser takes Marx's definition above and asks what Habermas's theory misses about gender — the hinge between the Frankfurt School and feminist theory. Paywalled on JSTOR.

  • Feminist Perspectives on Power — Amy Allen, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy Intermediate

    A free map of how feminist theory thinks about power — as a resource, as domination, as empowerment — with socialist, intersectional, poststructuralist and postcolonial approaches side by side.

  • Critical Race Theory: An Introduction (4th ed.) — Richard Delgado & Jean Stefancic, foreword by Angela Harris, NYU Press (2023) Beginner

    The standard primer on the legal movement, which grew out of critical legal studies rather than Frankfurt, and on its core ideas; the fourth edition also covers the recent political backlash against it. Paid.

  • Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex: A Black Feminist Critique of Antidiscrimination Doctrine, Feminist Theory and Antiracist Politics — Kimberlé Crenshaw, University of Chicago Legal Forum (1989) Intermediate

    The article that introduced "intersectionality", built on discrimination cases that failed Black women because courts looked at race and sex separately. Free; or start with her 2016 TED talk.

  • Orientalism — Edward W. Said, Vintage (1979; first 1978) Intermediate

    The founding text of postcolonial theory: how Western scholarship and literature invented "the Orient", read through Foucault's idea of discourse. Paid.

  • The End of Progress: Decolonizing the Normative Foundations of Critical Theory — Amy Allen, Columbia University Press (2016) Advanced

    Postcolonial theory turned on the Frankfurt School itself: Habermas, Honneth and Forst lean on ideas of historical progress that decolonial critics reject, and Allen rebuilds critique from Adorno and Foucault instead. Paid.

  • Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity — Judith Butler, Routledge Classics (2006; first 1990) Advanced

    Queer theory's founding book: gender as something repeatedly performed rather than the expression of an inner essence. Dense and much misread. Paid.

  • What is Critique? An Essay on Foucault's Virtue — Judith Butler, Raymond Williams Lecture (2000), transversal texts Advanced

    Butler — who received Frankfurt's Adorno Prize in 2012 — reading Foucault's lecture of the same name alongside Adorno and Raymond Williams: the clearest bridge between the Frankfurt tradition and the French one. Free.

  • Main Currents of Marxism: The Founders, The Golden Age, The Breakdown — Leszek Kołakowski, trans. P. S. Falla, W. W. Norton (2005; Polish 1976–78) Advanced

    The case against, from a former Marxist: the third part's chapters on the Frankfurt School and on Marcuse are a famously severe critique of the whole tradition. Paid, and over a thousand pages in one volume — go straight to those chapters.

  • Melancholy Science — Martin Jay, Harper's Magazine (July 2020) Beginner

    The school's leading historian on how "the Frankfurt School" became a far-right conspiracy theory about "cultural Marxism", recycled in the 2011 Oslo attacker's manifesto — useful for telling the scholarship from the slogan. From his collection Splinters in Your Eye; Harper's is metered.

  • Frankfurt School — Monoskop

    A free, heavily linked bibliography of the school and its members — the place to go when you want the next thing by an author and don't know what it is.

  • Structuralism and Post-Structuralism — on this site

    The French theory Habermas spent the 1980s arguing with — Foucault, Derrida and the rest — and the source of the ideas about language, power and the subject that the feminist, postcolonial and queer theory above builds on.
