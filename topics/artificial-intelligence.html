Artificial Intelligence

Last updated: 2026-07-30

Artificial intelligence is the study of building agents — things that perceive an environment and act in it to achieve goals. That one definition, which Russell and Norvig put at the front of Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach, is what holds an otherwise sprawling field together. A chess engine, a delivery robot, a spam filter and a chatbot are all agents; they differ in what they can observe, how much they know in advance, and whether the world is predictable enough to plan through.

From that frame the classical toolkit falls out in order: search when you know the rules and need a route to the goal, adversarial search when something is playing against you, constraint satisfaction and logic when the problem is better stated than explored, planning when actions have preconditions and effects, probability when you cannot see everything, and reinforcement learning when nobody can tell you the right answer but the world will eventually score you.

Learning is one technique inside that picture rather than the whole of it — a point worth keeping in view now that "AI" is often used to mean "a large language model." This page is the map and the classical core; the depth on statistical learning, deep networks, transformers, LLMs and image generation lives on the four sibling pages linked at the bottom.

Good to know first: comfortable programming in some language (Python for most course material), basic data structures and graph algorithms, and enough probability to be at ease with conditional probability and expectation. Linear algebra and calculus only matter once you reach the learning chapters — the maths here is lighter than on the machine learning and generative AI pages.

📚 Books

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans — Melanie Mitchell, Farrar, Straus and Giroux (2019) Beginner

    The best first book: a working researcher explaining what these systems actually do, what they don't, and why the field keeps over-promising. Paid, no free edition.

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach (4th ed.) — Stuart Russell & Peter Norvig, Pearson (2020) Intermediate

    The standard text for the whole field, and the spine of this page — agents, search, logic, planning, probability, learning, robotics. The book is paid, but the official site gives away the exercises, pseudocode, figures and errata for free.

  • Artificial Intelligence: Foundations of Computational Agents (3rd ed.) — David Poole & Alan Mackworth, Cambridge University Press (2023) Intermediate

    The free alternative to AIMA — full text online, same agent-centred structure, tighter and more current on representation and reasoning. Start here if you'd rather not buy anything.

  • Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (2nd ed.) — Richard Sutton & Andrew Barto, MIT Press (2018) Advanced

    The definitive RL text, free as a full PDF from the authors' page. Works up from bandits and MDPs to function approximation without ever hand-waving the derivations.

  • Acting, Planning, and Learning — Ghallab, Nau & Traverso, Cambridge University Press (2025) Advanced

    Free PDF from the authors, and the current reference on automated planning — the part of AI that AIMA can only sketch in two chapters.

  • The Quest for Artificial Intelligence — Nils J. Nilsson, Cambridge University Press (2010) Intermediate

    A free PDF history of the field written by someone who helped build it. Read it to understand why symbolic AI, expert systems and the AI winters happened in that order.

  • Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control — Stuart Russell, Viking (2019) Beginner

    AIMA's co-author arguing that the standard model — build a machine that optimises a fixed objective — is the wrong one. Paid; the case for uncertainty about human preferences is worth the money.

  • The Alignment Problem — Brian Christian, W. W. Norton (2020) Beginner

    Reported rather than argued: how bias, reward hacking and specification failures actually showed up in deployed systems. Paid, and the most concrete of the AI-safety books.

  • Paradigms of Artificial Intelligence Programming — Peter Norvig, Morgan Kaufmann (1992) Advanced

    Norvig rebuilds the classic AI programs — ELIZA, GPS, a Prolog interpreter — in Common Lisp. Now free under MIT licence, and still the best book on how symbolic AI was actually written.

📄 Papers & Primary References

  • Computing Machinery and Intelligence — Alan Turing, Mind 59 (1950) Beginner

    Where the field starts. Turing replaces "can machines think?" with the imitation game and then answers nine objections in plain English — genuinely readable seventy-five years on. Free copy hosted by Oxford; the journal version is paywalled.

  • A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence — McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester & Shannon (1955) Beginner

    The grant application that named the field, and proposed to make substantial progress on it over one summer with ten people. Short, and a useful calibration on AI timelines.

  • Intelligence Without Representation — Rodney Brooks, Artificial Intelligence 47 (1991) Intermediate

    The attack that broke symbolic AI's monopoly: build robots as layered reactive behaviours, and skip the world model entirely. The clearest statement of the case against the sense–model–plan–act pipeline.

  • Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning — Mnih et al., DeepMind / Nature (2015) Advanced

    Deep Q-Networks: one architecture learning forty-nine Atari games from raw pixels and score alone. The paper that made deep RL a research programme. Paywalled at Nature.

  • Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search — Silver et al., DeepMind / Nature (2016) Advanced

    AlphaGo — the clearest demonstration that classical search and learned evaluation are complementary, not rivals. Monte Carlo tree search guided by policy and value networks. Paywalled at Nature.

  • Highly accurate protein structure prediction with AlphaFold — Jumper et al., DeepMind / Nature (2021) Advanced

    Open access, and the strongest case that AI can close a genuine scientific problem rather than a benchmark. Read it when you want an answer to "what has this actually been good for?"

  • Concrete Problems in AI Safety — Amodei, Olah, Steinhardt, Christiano, Schulman & Mané (2016) Intermediate

    Turns AI safety from speculation into five engineering problems — side effects, reward hacking, scalable oversight, safe exploration, distributional shift. Still the standard framing.

  • Why AI is Harder Than We Think — Melanie Mitchell (2021) Intermediate

    Four fallacies behind seventy years of over-confident predictions, including the assumption that narrow progress scales to general intelligence. The best short antidote to hype in either direction.

✍️ Articles & Blog Posts

  • Introduction to the A* Algorithm — Amit Patel, Red Blob Games Beginner

    Breadth-first to Dijkstra to A*, each step an interactive diagram you can drag. The fastest way to make heuristic search click, and the standard reference for anyone implementing it.

  • The Bitter Lesson — Rich Sutton (2019) Intermediate

    Eight hundred words arguing that hand-built human knowledge loses to general methods plus compute, every time. The most-cited essay in modern AI, and the field's own explanation of why it changed shape.

  • On Chomsky and the Two Cultures of Statistical Learning — Peter Norvig (2011) Intermediate

    Norvig answering Chomsky's charge that statistical models aren't science. The clearest account of the split between AI as understanding and AI as engineering — the argument underneath the whole symbolic-versus-statistical story.

  • Artificial Intelligence — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy Advanced

    Book-length and rigorous on the questions engineering texts skip: what would count as intelligence, what the Turing test does and doesn't establish, strong versus weak AI.

🎥 Videos & Talks

  • AlphaGo — The Movie — Google DeepMind (2020) Beginner

    The full documentary, free. No technical prerequisites, and the best ninety minutes for understanding what a search-plus-learning system feels like from the outside.

  • MIT 6.034: Artificial Intelligence — Lecture Videos — Patrick Winston, MIT OpenCourseWare (Fall 2010) Intermediate

    Winston's complete course, free. Pre-deep-learning and better for it — search, constraints, logic and learning taught by someone who thought teaching was the point.

  • CS 188 (Artificial Intelligence) at UC Berkeley — UC Berkeley Intermediate

    Recorded lectures for the course below, updated each semester. The modern counterpart to 6.034 if you want current framing and worked exam-style examples.

  • Introduction to Reinforcement Learning — David Silver, DeepMind × UCL (2015) Advanced

    Ten lectures from AlphaGo's lead researcher, tracking Sutton & Barto chapter by chapter. Still the standard video course on RL despite its age.

  • 3 principles for creating safer AI — Stuart Russell, TED (2017) Beginner

    Twenty minutes on why optimising a fixed objective is the wrong design, from the person who wrote the textbook teaching it. The short version of Human Compatible.

🎓 Courses & Tutorials

  • Elements of AI — University of Helsinki & MinnaLearn Beginner

    Free, no programming required, and honest about limitations. The right starting point if you want the concepts — search, probability, learning — before deciding how deep to go.

  • CS50's Introduction to Artificial Intelligence with Python — Brian Yu & David Malan, Harvard Beginner

    Seven weeks of projects — minimax tic-tac-toe, a knowledge-base puzzle solver, PageRank, a Minesweeper agent. Free to take; only the certificate costs money.

  • CS 188: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence — UC Berkeley (Spring 2026) Intermediate

    Slides, notes and the famous Pac-Man projects, all public — you implement A*, minimax with alpha-beta, and Q-learning against a real game. The best free hands-on route through AIMA.

  • Intro to Artificial Intelligence — Peter Norvig & Sebastian Thrun, Udacity Intermediate

    The 2011 Stanford course that started the MOOC era, taught by AIMA's co-author and the leader of Stanford's DARPA Grand Challenge team. Free to audit.

  • CS221: Artificial Intelligence — Principles and Techniques — Stanford University Advanced

    Public slides, notes and assignments, organised around the models rather than the applications — search, MDPs, games, CSPs, logic. Heavier on maths than CS 188.

  • Spinning Up in Deep RL — Joshua Achiam, OpenAI Advanced

    Free, and unusually practical: the theory you actually need, clean reference implementations of the key algorithms, and honest advice on why your RL run isn't converging.

🔧 Tools & Interactive

  • AIMA JavaScript Visualizations — aimacode, in-browser

    Watch AIMA's algorithms run, chapter by chapter, with no setup. Search expanding a frontier is far easier to understand animated than in pseudocode.

  • aima-python — aimacode, official book code

    Every algorithm in the textbook implemented in Python, with a Jupyter notebook per chapter. The reference to read when the pseudocode is ambiguous.

  • Gymnasium — Farama Foundation (maintained fork of OpenAI Gym)

    The standard environment API for reinforcement learning. Gives you CartPole, Atari and MuJoCo behind one interface, so you can test an agent instead of building a simulator.

🔗 Further Reading / Going Deeper

  • The 2026 AI Index Report — Stanford HAI

    Free annual audit of the field in numbers — capability benchmarks, compute, investment, policy. Use it to check any claim about where AI currently stands.

  • One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence (AI100) — Stanford University

    A century-long study issuing a panel report every five years on AI's effects on society. The long view, written by researchers rather than commentators.

  • Machine Learning — on this site

    The learning chapters, properly: fitting models to data, honest evaluation, and getting them to survive production.

  • Transformers (Deep Learning) — on this site

    The architecture that carried the statistical turn described above into every modality at once.

  • Large Language Models — on this site

    What most people now mean by "AI" — next-token prediction, alignment, prompting, retrieval and agents.

  • Generative AI (Beyond LLMs) — on this site

    Diffusion, GANs and the rest of the generative family — images, audio, video and 3D.
