Genetic Algorithms and Genetic Programming

Last updated: 2026-09-25

A genetic algorithm (GA) solves a problem by breeding answers rather than deriving one. It keeps a population of candidate solutions, scores each with a fitness function, and lets the better ones reproduce: pieces of two parents are recombined (crossover), small random changes are added (mutation), and over many generations the population drifts toward good solutions. John Holland worked the idea out at the University of Michigan in the 1960s and 70s. Genetic programming (GP), popularised by John Koza in 1992, runs the same loop on computer programs themselves — usually expression trees — so what evolves is a formula or a piece of code rather than a list of numbers.

The appeal is how little either one assumes. There is no gradient to compute and no model of the problem to write, only a way to score a candidate, so they work where calculus-based optimisation can't: schedules, engineering designs, game controllers, several conflicting objectives at once. The price is thousands of evaluations and no guarantee of finding the optimum — and the No Free Lunch theorems make precise that no optimiser wins on every problem. The payoffs are real all the same: an evolved antenna flew on NASA's ST5 mission in 2006, GP-based symbolic regression recovers readable equations from data, and since 2023 FunSearch and AlphaEvolve have put a language model in the mutation step to discover new algorithms.

This page starts with the classic GA and GP, then follows them into neuroevolution, evolution strategies, open-ended search and LLM-guided program evolution.

Good to know first: comfortable programming (Python for most tutorials), basic probability (random sampling, distributions), and the idea of an optimisation problem — a score to maximise over a space of candidates. Trees and recursion help for genetic programming. Calculus and neural networks only matter for the evolution-strategies and neuroevolution material.

📚 Books

  • An Introduction to Genetic Algorithms — Melanie Mitchell, MIT Press (1996) Beginner

    The classic first book: short, clear, and serious about both sides of the field — GAs as problem solvers and as models of evolution — with thought and computer exercises throughout. Paid.

  • Essentials of Metaheuristics (2nd ed.) — Sean Luke, Lulu (2013, online version still updated) Beginner

    Free PDF written for programmers rather than theorists: hill climbing and simulated annealing, then evolution strategies, the GA and tree-based GP, every algorithm as plain pseudocode. The quickest free overview of the whole toolkit.

  • Hands-On Genetic Algorithms with Python (2nd ed.) — Eyal Wirsansky, Packt (2024) Intermediate

    Applied GAs in Python with DEAP: scheduling and routing problems first, then feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, architecture search and reinforcement learning. Paid; all the code is free on GitHub.

  • Introduction to Evolutionary Computing (2nd ed.) — A. E. Eiben & J. E. Smith, Springer (2015) Intermediate

    The standard textbook for the field as a whole: GAs, evolution strategies, evolutionary programming and GP as dialects of one framework, then parameter control, multi-objective search, constraints and theory. Paid.

  • A Field Guide to Genetic Programming — Riccardo Poli, William B. Langdon & Nicholas F. McPhee (2008) Intermediate

    The GP book to read first: tree-based GP from scratch, then linear, graph and grammar-based variants, bloat, and a tour of real applications. Free as a Creative Commons PDF, with a small GP system (TinyGP) in an appendix.

  • Algorithms for Optimization — Mykel J. Kochenderfer & Tim A. Wheeler, MIT Press (2019) Intermediate

    Puts GAs where they belong — one chapter of population methods beside gradient descent, direct search and surrogate models — which is what you need to decide whether a GA is the right tool at all. Free PDF with Julia code; a second edition is due in January 2027.

  • Genetic Algorithms in Search, Optimization, and Machine Learning — David E. Goldberg, Addison-Wesley (1989) Intermediate

    The book that took GAs mainstream, by Holland's student: schema theory, the building-block hypothesis and complete implementations (in Pascal). Dated in places, still the most-cited GA text. Paid.

  • Genetic Programming: On the Programming of Computers by Means of Natural Selection — John R. Koza, MIT Press (1992) Advanced

    The founding text of GP: 800-plus pages of programs bred to solve problems from symbolic regression to robot control, with the method laid out in full. Paid, and more reference than read-through.

  • Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems — John H. Holland, MIT Press (1992; first published 1975) Advanced

    Where it began: the GA as a general model of adaptation, and the schema theorem arguing that recombining good building blocks is why it works. Dense and mathematical — read it for the ideas, not the practice. Paid.

  • Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned: The Myth of the Objective — Kenneth O. Stanley & Joel Lehman, Springer (2015) Beginner

    The lesson of novelty search, written for anyone: ambitious objectives are deceptive, so collecting interesting stepping stones often beats chasing the goal directly. Paid.

📄 Papers & Primary References

  • Genetic Algorithms — John H. Holland, Scientific American 267(1) (1992) Beginner

    The inventor's own popular account of how selection and recombination find solutions nobody designed. Paywalled in the Scientific American archive; Holland's free Scholarpedia article below covers the same ground.

  • The Surprising Creativity of Digital Evolution — Lehman, Clune, Misevic et al., Artificial Life 26(2) (2020) Beginner

    Dozens of researchers' stories of evolution exploiting simulator bugs, gaming fitness functions and finding answers nobody expected. The best short read on what these algorithms actually do. Free.

  • A Genetic Algorithm Tutorial — Darrell Whitley, Statistics and Computing 4(2) (1994) Intermediate

    The standard tutorial paper: the canonical GA, the schema theorem, exact models and parallel island GAs, precise without being heavy. Free from Whitley's research group at Colorado State.

  • Hierarchical Genetic Algorithms Operating on Populations of Computer Programs — John R. Koza, IJCAI (1989) Intermediate

    Koza's first paper on breeding programs as trees of LISP expressions — the method his 1992 book scaled up, building on Nichael Cramer's 1985 tree representation. Free from Koza's site as his manuscript version.

  • Automated Antenna Design with Evolutionary Algorithms — Hornby, Globus, Linden & Lohn, AIAA Space (2006) Intermediate

    How evolution designed the oddly bent wire antenna for NASA's Space Technology 5 spacecraft — the standard example of an evolved design meeting a real engineering specification. Free on NASA's technical reports server.

  • No Free Lunch Theorems for Optimization — David H. Wolpert & William G. Macready, IEEE Transactions on Evolutionary Computation 1(1) (1997) Advanced

    Averaged over every possible problem, all optimisers perform equally well. The result that explains why a GA is only as good as its fit to your problem. Paywalled at IEEE.

  • A Fast and Elitist Multiobjective Genetic Algorithm: NSGA-II — Deb, Pratap, Agarwal & Meyarivan, IEEE Transactions on Evolutionary Computation 6(2) (2002) Advanced

    The default GA for several objectives at once — fast non-dominated sorting, crowding distance and elitism — and one of the most-cited papers in the field. Paywalled at IEEE; pymoo and DEAP below both implement it.

  • Evolving Neural Networks through Augmenting Topologies — Kenneth O. Stanley & Risto Miikkulainen, Evolutionary Computation 10(2) (2002) Advanced

    NEAT: evolve a network's structure along with its weights, starting minimal and growing, with speciation to protect new innovations. The founding paper of modern neuroevolution; free from the authors' lab.

  • Distilling Free-Form Natural Laws from Experimental Data — Michael Schmidt & Hod Lipson, Science 324 (2009) Advanced

    Evolutionary symbolic regression recovers Hamiltonians, Lagrangians and conservation laws from motion-tracking data of oscillators and double pendulums, with no physics built in. The landmark for GP as a scientific tool. Paywalled at Science.

  • Abandoning Objectives: Evolution Through the Search for Novelty Alone — Joel Lehman & Kenneth O. Stanley, Evolutionary Computation 19(2) (2011) Advanced

    Reward only new behaviour, ignore the goal, and search can beat objective-driven evolution on deceptive problems such as maze navigation. The paper behind novelty search and quality diversity; free from UCF's institutional repository.

  • Deep Neuroevolution: Genetic Algorithms Are a Competitive Alternative for Training Deep Neural Networks for Reinforcement Learning — Such, Madhavan, Conti, Lehman, Stanley & Clune, Uber AI Labs (2017) Advanced

    A plain GA with no gradients evolves networks of over four million parameters for Atari and humanoid locomotion, and trains faster than DQN or A3C. Read it with OpenAI's evolution strategies paper, which scaled ES to over a thousand workers the same year.

  • Mathematical discoveries from program search with large language models — Romera-Paredes et al., Google DeepMind / Nature (2023) Advanced

    FunSearch: a pretrained LLM proposes program edits, an evaluator scores them, and an evolutionary loop keeps the best — finding new cap-set constructions and better bin-packing heuristics. Open access; the code and discovered programs are on GitHub.

✍️ Articles & Blog Posts

  • Genetic algorithms — John H. Holland, Scholarpedia (2012) Beginner

    A short, free overview from the person who invented the method: the basic loop, the building-block intuition behind it, and where it has been used.

  • Simple Genetic Algorithm From Scratch in Python — Jason Brownlee, Machine Learning Mastery (2021) Beginner

    A complete GA in a page of Python — first on OneMax bit strings, then on a continuous function — so every operator is code you can read and change.

  • Neuroevolution: A different kind of deep learning — Kenneth O. Stanley, O'Reilly Radar (2017) Beginner

    NEAT's co-author on why you might evolve a network instead of training it, and what evolution offers that gradient descent doesn't: structure search, diversity and open-endedness.

  • A Visual Guide to Evolution Strategies — David Ha (2017) Intermediate

    Animated populations searching toy landscapes: a simple GA, then evolution strategies, CMA-ES and OpenAI's ES. The fastest route to an intuition for population-based search over continuous parameters.

  • Evolution Strategies — Lilian Weng, Lil'Log (2019) Advanced

    The maths behind that intuition — Gaussian ES, CMA-ES and natural evolution strategies — and how each is used in deep reinforcement learning.

  • Using Evolutionary AutoML to Discover Neural Network Architectures — Esteban Real, Google Research (2018) Intermediate

    Evolution searching image-classifier architectures at scale; the regularized ("ageing") evolution paper behind it held its own against reinforcement-learning search and produced the state-of-the-art AmoebaNets.

  • AutoML-Zero: Evolving Code that Learns — Esteban Real & Chen Liang, Google Research (2020) Intermediate

    GP-style search starting from basic maths operations rediscovered linear regression and two-layer networks trained with backpropagation. The ICML 2020 paper has the details.

  • AlphaEvolve: A Gemini-powered coding agent for designing advanced algorithms — Google DeepMind (2025) Beginner

    FunSearch scaled up to whole codebases: it multiplied 4×4 complex matrices with 48 multiplications, beating Strassen's 1969 algorithm, and recovers about 0.7% of Google's compute in data-centre scheduling. Technical paper.

🎥 Videos & Talks

  • Lecture 13: Learning: Genetic Algorithms — Patrick Winston, MIT 6.034 (Fall 2010) Beginner

    One lecture that builds the GA from the ground up and ends on why ranking candidates by diversity as well as fitness keeps a population from stalling. Free on OpenCourseWare.

  • Evolved Virtual Creatures — Karl Sims (1994), SIGGRAPH collection on the Internet Archive Beginner

    Block creatures whose bodies and brains were both evolved, learning to swim, walk, jump and fight over a cube. Still the most vivid demonstration there is; the SIGGRAPH '94 paper explains how.

  • MarI/O — Machine Learning for Video Games — SethBling (2015) Beginner

    NEAT learning a Super Mario World level from nothing, with the evolving network drawn on screen as it plays. The neuroevolution demo that brought NEAT to a general audience.

  • Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned — Kenneth O. Stanley, TTI/Vanguard talk Beginner

    The ideas behind novelty search as a talk: why objectives deceive, and what search looks like without one. Reposted by Stanley on his own channel after the original was taken down.

  • The Next Great Scientific Theory is Hiding Inside a Neural Network — Miles Cranmer, Simons Foundation (2024) Intermediate

    Train a neural network, then use evolutionary symbolic regression to distil it into an equation a scientist can read. The case for evolved formulas in modern science, from the author of PySR.

🎓 Courses & Tutorials

  • The Nature of Code — Chapter 9: Evolutionary Computing — Daniel Shiffman, No Starch Press (2nd ed., 2024) Beginner

    Free online and runnable in the browser: a GA that types Shakespeare, then smart rockets, interactive selection and an evolving ecosystem. Pairs with the Coding Train video series on the same chapter.

  • Introduction to Complexity — Melanie Mitchell, Santa Fe Institute / Complexity Explorer (2023) Beginner

    Free and self-paced with no prerequisites; Unit 5 teaches genetic algorithms through Robby the Robot, a can-collecting robot whose strategy is evolved rather than programmed, with a NetLogo model to run yourself.

  • Introduction to GP — Trevor Stephens, gplearn documentation Intermediate

    The clearest walk through GP's moving parts — program trees, tournaments, subtree, hoist and point mutation, and bloat control with a parsimony penalty — tied to a scikit-learn-compatible library you can run straight away.

🔧 Tools & Interactive

  • HTML5 Genetic Algorithm 2D Car Thingy — rednuht.org, in the browser

    Watch a GA breed two-wheeled cars over random terrain, and change the mutation rate, mutation size and number of elite clones mid-run. A shared seed gives everyone the same track.

  • DEAP — Université Laval, Python

    The standard Python framework for evolutionary computation: GAs, GP, evolution strategies and NSGA-II assembled from small parts, with a GP tutorial in the docs. Described in JMLR (2012).

  • PyGAD — Ahmed Gad, Python

    The quickest way to run a GA on your own fitness function, single- or multi-objective. It can also evolve the weights of Keras and PyTorch models.

  • PySR — Miles Cranmer, Python with a Julia engine

    High-performance symbolic regression: a multi-population evolutionary search over equations that returns a readable formula instead of a black box. Described in this paper.

  • TPOT — Epistasis Lab, Python

    Genetic programming over whole machine learning pipelines — preprocessing, model and hyperparameters — as an AutoML tool. Recently rewritten with multi-objective search; the original paper explains the idea.

  • pymoo — Julian Blank & Kalyanmoy Deb, Python

    Multi-objective optimisation done properly: NSGA-II, NSGA-III and their relatives, plus the Pareto-front plots and decision-making tools you need to read the results.

  • neat-python — CodeReclaimers, Python

    A pure-Python NEAT small enough to read, with a walkthrough of the classic XOR example to start from.

  • ShinkaEvolve — Sakana AI, Python

    Open-source LLM-driven program evolution in the AlphaEvolve mould, built to need far fewer evaluations. Apache-licensed; paper (2025).

🔗 Further Reading / Going Deeper

  • The CMA Evolution Strategy: A Tutorial — Nikolaus Hansen (2016)

    The method to reach for once your problem is continuous: an evolution strategy that learns the shape of the landscape as it searches, explained by its inventor. pycma is his reference implementation.

  • Quality Diversity: A New Frontier for Evolutionary Computation — Justin K. Pugh, Lisa B. Soros & Kenneth O. Stanley, Frontiers in Robotics and AI (2016)

    Where novelty search led: algorithms that return a whole map of diverse, high-performing solutions instead of one winner. Open access; MAP-Elites is the method to try first.

  • Evolution through Large Models — Joel Lehman et al. (2022)

    An early proposal to use a code-trained language model as GP's mutation operator — the idea FunSearch and AlphaEvolve went on to scale.

  • Contemporary Symbolic Regression Methods and their Relative Performance — La Cava et al., NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks (2021)

    SRBench: 14 symbolic regression methods and 7 machine learning baselines on 252 problems. The best on real-world data combined genetic algorithms with parameter estimation — the place to check any symbolic regression claim.

  • Specification gaming examples in AI — Victoria Krakovna (maintained list)

    A running catalogue of systems that satisfy the letter of their objective and miss the point, many of the classics from evolutionary algorithms. Read it before writing your first fitness function.

  • Annual "Humies" Awards for Human-Competitive Results — held at GECCO each year since 2004

    Prizes for results from genetic and evolutionary computation that compete with human work — patentable inventions, new records, flown hardware. The best index of what the field can actually do.

  • The Genetic Programming Bibliography — William B. Langdon et al.

    Some 19,000 GP references, searchable by author and subject. Where to look once you know what you're looking for.

  • Artificial Intelligence — on this site

    The parent field. GAs sit in its local-search chapter beside hill climbing and simulated annealing, and evolution strategies compete directly with its reinforcement learning.

  • Machine Learning — on this site

    The evaluation discipline GP needs once it is fitting data — held-out tests, overfitting, and the gradient-boosted trees any evolved formula has to beat.

  • Deep Learning — on this site

    The networks neuroevolution evolves, and the gradient-based training it is usually up against.

  • Large Language Models — on this site

    The models doing the mutating in FunSearch and AlphaEvolve — how they are trained, and why their code proposals are good enough to evolve.
