Deep Learning

Last updated: 2026-07-30

Deep learning builds models out of many stacked layers of simple, differentiable operations, and trains the whole stack at once against a single loss. The word that does the work is learned: instead of an engineer deciding which features matter — edges, word counts, hand-tuned filters — the network discovers its own intermediate representations, each layer composing the one below it into something more abstract.

The mechanism underneath every model on this page is the same three-part loop. Define a differentiable function with millions of adjustable weights, score its output with a loss, then use backpropagation — the chain rule applied efficiently in reverse — to work out how each weight should move, and take a small step. Everything else is a variation on that theme: what shape the function takes, how you keep the gradients healthy, and how you stop it memorising the training set.

The ideas are old — backprop was published in 1986, convolutional nets in 1998 — but the field only became inevitable in 2012, when a GPU-trained network halved the ImageNet error rate and made it obvious that depth plus data plus compute beat feature engineering. That recipe has since absorbed vision, speech, language, protein folding and image generation, with surprisingly few changes to the core loop.

This page is the end-to-end route: the fundamentals first, then convolutional and recurrent networks, then the attention-based architectures that took over from both. Once you reach the far end, the four sibling pages linked at the bottom pick up individual branches in far more depth than a single page can.

Good to know first: Python and NumPy, linear algebra up to comfortable matrix multiplication, and calculus through partial derivatives and the chain rule — the chain rule especially, since backpropagation is the chain rule. Basic probability helps for loss functions. The maths floor here is genuinely higher than on the artificial intelligence page; if classical models and evaluation are still shaky, the machine learning page is the better first stop.

📚 Books

  • Neural Networks and Deep Learning — Michael Nielsen (2015) Beginner

    Free online, and still the clearest explanation of why backpropagation works rather than just what it computes. Builds a digit classifier from scratch in plain Python. Note the site is HTTP-only.

  • The Little Book of Deep Learning — François Fleuret, University of Geneva (2023) Beginner

    A free 160-page PDF formatted to read on a phone. The fastest honest overview of the whole field if you already have the maths and want the shape of it in an evening.

  • Dive into Deep Learning — Zhang, Lipton, Li & Smola, Cambridge University Press Beginner

    Free, and every equation comes with runnable code in PyTorch, JAX and TensorFlow. Adopted at 500 universities; the best book to work through with an interpreter open beside you.

  • Understanding Deep Learning — Simon J.D. Prince, MIT Press (2023) Intermediate

    Free PDF from the author, with notebooks and slides. The best-illustrated modern textbook — it covers transformers, diffusion and RL rather than stopping at 2016.

  • Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts — Christopher M. Bishop & Hugh Bishop, Springer (2024) Intermediate

    Free to read online. Bishop's successor to Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning, and the most careful treatment of the probabilistic reasoning behind the architectures.

  • Deep Learning with Python (3rd ed.) — François Chollet & Matthew Watson, Manning (2025) Intermediate

    Paid, and worth it: Keras's creator on how to actually build things, now covering Keras 3 across PyTorch and JAX plus generative models. Strong on judgement, not just syntax.

  • Deep Learning — Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, MIT Press (2016) Advanced

    Free in full as HTML. Predates transformers and reads as history on architectures, but Part I on the mathematical foundations and Part II on optimisation and regularisation are still the reference.

📄 Papers & Primary References

  • Learning representations by back-propagating errors — Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, Nature 323 (1986) Intermediate

    Four pages that made the whole field possible, and the origin of "representations you didn't design". Free PDF from Hinton's page; the Nature version is paywalled.

  • Long Short-Term Memory — Sepp Hochreiter & Jürgen Schmidhuber, Neural Computation 9 (1997) Advanced

    The gated cell that solved vanishing gradients and ran sequence modelling for the next twenty years. Free copy hosted by CMU; the journal version is paywalled.

  • Gradient-Based Learning Applied to Document Recognition — LeCun, Bottou, Bengio & Haffner, Proceedings of the IEEE (1998) Advanced

    LeNet-5, and the paper that established convolution, pooling and weight sharing as the way to handle images. It was reading cheques commercially fourteen years before ImageNet. HTTP-only host.

  • ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks — Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, NeurIPS (2012) Intermediate

    AlexNet — the result that ended the argument. Free from the proceedings, and short enough to read in a sitting: ReLUs, dropout, two GPUs, and a ten-point drop in error.

  • Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting — Srivastava, Hinton, Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Salakhutdinov, JMLR 15 (2014) Intermediate

    Open access. Randomly deleting half your neurons during training sounds like sabotage and works like an ensemble — the clearest example of how odd good regularisation looks.

  • Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization — Diederik P. Kingma & Jimmy Ba, ICLR (2015) Intermediate

    The optimiser you will use by default for years without thinking about it. Worth reading once so the defaults — β₁, β₂, ε — stop being magic numbers.

  • Batch Normalization — Sergey Ioffe & Christian Szegedy, ICML (2015) Intermediate

    Made deep networks trainable at higher learning rates and turned normalisation into a standard layer. The paper's own explanation has since been challenged — the technique works regardless.

  • Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition — He, Zhang, Ren & Sun, CVPR (2016) Intermediate

    ResNet. The skip connection is one line of code and it is why networks can be 100+ layers deep — including, later, every transformer. Possibly the highest ratio of impact to complexity in the field.

  • Deep learning — LeCun, Bengio & Hinton, Nature 521 (2015) Beginner

    The three Turing laureates summarising the field for a general scientific audience. The best single-sitting overview of the first wave. Free PDF from Hinton's page; the Nature version is paywalled.

  • Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization — Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht & Vinyals, ICLR (2017) Advanced

    Networks can perfectly memorise randomly-labelled data and still generalise on real data — which breaks the textbook capacity-based explanation. Read it once you're comfortable, to know what the field genuinely doesn't understand.

  • Attention Is All You Need — Vaswani et al., NeurIPS (2017) Advanced

    The break with recurrence: drop the sequential dependency, attend over the whole sequence at once, and the model becomes parallelisable enough to scale. The transformers page takes this apart properly.

  • Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models — Ho, Jain & Abbeel, NeurIPS (2020) Advanced

    The other branch of the modern family: train a network to reverse gradual noising and you get a generative model. Included here for the map — the generative AI page goes deeper.

✍️ Articles & Blog Posts

  • Yes you should understand backprop — Andrej Karpathy (2016) Beginner

    Why autograd is a leaky abstraction, in four concrete failures: saturated sigmoids, dead ReLUs, exploding RNN gradients, and a clipping bug in a published DQN. Read this before you trust a framework.

  • Understanding LSTM Networks — Christopher Olah (2015) Intermediate

    The diagrams everyone else copies. Walks the cell state through each gate in turn and makes an intimidating equation set feel obvious.

  • The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks — Andrej Karpathy (2015) Intermediate

    A character-level RNN learning to produce Shakespeare, LaTeX and Linux source. Historically the post that convinced a lot of people sequence models were going somewhere — and the direct ancestor of today's language models.

  • Why Momentum Really Works — Gabriel Goh, Distill (2017) Intermediate

    Interactive throughout: drag the momentum term and watch optimisation paths change. The clearest account of why plain gradient descent struggles and what momentum actually fixes.

  • A Recipe for Training Neural Networks — Andrej Karpathy (2019) Intermediate

    "Neural net training fails silently." A six-stage process — inspect the data, get a dumb baseline, overfit deliberately, then regularise — that will save you more time than any architecture choice.

  • Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits — Olah, Cammarata, Schubert, Goh, Petrov & Carter, Distill (2020) Advanced

    Opens up a trained vision model and finds curve detectors and other interpretable features wired into recognisable circuits. The founding argument for mechanistic interpretability.

🎥 Videos & Talks

  • Neural networks — 3Blue1Brown Beginner

    Start here. Grant Sanderson animates what a layer does, what gradient descent is searching, and what backprop is really computing — the visual intuition every other resource assumes you already have.

  • Neural Networks: Zero to Hero — Andrej Karpathy Intermediate

    Free, and the best hands-on course in the field: eight long videos building up from a scalar autograd engine to a working GPT, typing every line. Nothing is imported that hasn't been explained.

  • MIT 6.S191: Introduction to Deep Learning — Alexander Amini & Ava Amini, MIT Beginner

    MIT's intensive bootcamp, re-recorded every year and posted free — so the material stays current in a way most university lecture series don't. Labs at introtodeeplearning.com.

  • CS231n: Convolutional Neural Networks for Visual Recognition (Spring 2017) — Fei-Fei Li, Justin Johnson & Serena Yeung, Stanford Intermediate

    The complete lecture collection, free. Dated on the newest architectures but unmatched on convolution, backprop through a computational graph, and training mechanics.

  • Stanford CS230: Deep Learning — Andrew Ng & Kian Katanforoosh, Stanford (Autumn 2018) Intermediate

    The free lecture recordings for Stanford's deep learning course. Strongest on the part courses usually skip: how to structure a project, diagnose a bad model, and choose what to try next.

🎓 Courses & Tutorials

  • Practical Deep Learning for Coders — Jeremy Howard, fast.ai Beginner

    Free, and deliberately top-down: you train and deploy a working model in lesson one, and the theory arrives once you care about it. The right course if you can code but the maths intimidates you.

  • Deep Learning Specialization — Andrew Ng, DeepLearning.AI on Coursera Beginner

    Five courses, bottom-up, building the fundamentals by hand before reaching for a framework. Paid subscription with no free audit, though Coursera offers financial aid — the CS230 recordings above cover much of the same ground free.

  • CS231n Course Notes — Stanford University Intermediate

    Free and famous in their own right. The notes on backpropagation and on optimisation are the ones people send you when you say you don't quite follow the gradient maths.

  • NYU Deep Learning (DS-GA 1008) — Yann LeCun & Alfredo Canziani, NYU Advanced

    Free lectures, notes and notebooks from a Turing laureate, with an unusually strong thread on energy-based and self-supervised learning that other courses barely mention.

  • PyTorch: Learn the Basics — official PyTorch tutorials Beginner

    Tensors, autograd, datasets and a training loop in one sitting. Do this early — nearly every paper implementation and course exercise you meet will be PyTorch.

  • UvA Deep Learning Tutorials — University of Amsterdam Intermediate

    Free, unusually rigorous notebooks in both PyTorch and JAX, covering initialisation, optimisers, normalisation and attention. Good for filling the gaps a top-down course leaves behind.

🔧 Tools & Interactive

  • TensorFlow Playground — in-browser, no setup

    Add layers, change the activation, break the learning rate, and watch the decision boundary respond in real time. Ten minutes here teaches more about capacity and overfitting than a chapter.

  • micrograd — Andrej Karpathy, scalar autograd engine

    A complete reverse-mode autograd engine in about 100 lines, plus a small neural net library on top. Read it once and backpropagation stops being magic.

  • CNN Explainer — Polo Club of Data Science, Georgia Tech

    Click any neuron in a live convolutional network and see the exact patch and kernel that produced it. The best answer to "but what is the convolution actually doing here?"

  • PyTorch — the default research framework

    What almost everything is written in. Worth installing early even if you're following a Keras-based book, because the code you find on GitHub will assume it.

  • Netron — model architecture viewer, runs in-browser

    Drop in an ONNX, PyTorch or Keras file and get a navigable graph of the network with every layer's shapes. Invaluable when a downloaded model doesn't match the paper you're reading.

🔗 Further Reading / Going Deeper

  • Deep Learning Tuning Playbook — Godbole, Dahl, Gilmer, Shallue & Nado, Google Research

    Free, and the accumulated hyperparameter judgement that normally takes years to acquire — search strategy, batch size, when a result is real and when it's noise.

  • The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis — Jonathan Frankle & Michael Carbin, ICLR (2019)

    Dense networks contain small subnetworks that train to the same accuracy alone — if you know the initialisation. A good entry point into pruning, sparsity and why over-parameterisation helps.

  • Distill — archive, on hiatus since 2021

    No longer publishing, but the back catalogue of interactive explanations — momentum, feature visualisation, circuits, RNN memory — remains some of the best expository work the field has produced.

  • Machine Learning — on this site

    The ground floor: how to fit a model to data, evaluate it honestly, and know when a boosted tree would have beaten your network.

  • Artificial Intelligence — on this site

    The wider field this sits inside — agents, search, logic, planning and reinforcement learning, where learning is one technique among several.

  • Transformers — on this site

    The architecture sketched above, taken apart properly: attention, positional encoding, the KV cache, and what a current model actually does.

  • Large Language Models — on this site

    Where the sequence-modelling thread ends up — pretraining, alignment, prompting, retrieval and agents.

  • Generative AI (Beyond LLMs) — on this site

    The other branch from the diffusion paper above — images, audio, video and 3D, plus the GANs and VAEs that came before.
