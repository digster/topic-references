Large Language Models

Last updated: 2026-07-30

A large language model is a neural network trained on an enormous amount of text to do one deceptively simple thing: predict the next token. Do that well enough, at enough scale, and the model has to internalise grammar, facts, styles of argument and a working amount of world knowledge along the way. A second stage — post-training, where the model is fine-tuned on demonstrations and then shaped by human or AI preference signals — turns that raw predictor into something that answers questions and follows instructions instead of merely continuing your sentence.

What makes this worth studying is the collapse of the old pipeline. Where you once trained a separate model per task, one pretrained model now handles translation, summarising, classification and code by being asked, in plain language. That shifts the engineering problem: less architecture design, more prompting, retrieval, tool use, evaluation and cost. This page follows that arc end to end — how the models work, how they are trained and aligned, and how to build something reliable on top of one. It stays on language models throughout; image, audio and video generation are a separate topic.

Good to know first: comfortable Python, and enough neural-network familiarity to know what a layer, a loss and a gradient are. No transformer knowledge is assumed — several resources below build it up from nothing.

📚 Books

  • Co-Intelligence: Living and Working with AI — Ethan Mollick, Portfolio (2024) Beginner

    No maths at all — it's about what these systems are actually good and bad at, and how to work with one. Paid; the gentlest possible entry point.

  • Hands-On Large Language Models — Jay Alammar & Maarten Grootendorst, O'Reilly (2024) Beginner

    Nearly a thousand illustrations doing the explaining — the best visual on-ramp there is. Paid, but the notebooks are free on the book's site.

  • Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch) — Sebastian Raschka, Manning (2024) Intermediate

    Tokenizer, attention, pretraining loop, then fine-tuning — typed out in PyTorch with nothing hidden behind a library. Paid, but every notebook is free at rasbt/LLMs-from-scratch.

  • Foundations of Large Language Models — Tong Xiao & Jingbo Zhu (2025) Intermediate

    Free book-length treatment organised exactly as the field is: pre-training, generative models, prompting, alignment, inference.

  • Speech and Language Processing (3rd ed. draft) — Dan Jurafsky & James H. Martin Intermediate

    The standard NLP textbook, free in draft and steadily rewritten around LLMs — read it when you want the academic grounding under the hype.

  • AI Engineering: Building Applications with Foundation Models — Chip Huyen, O'Reilly (2025) Intermediate

    The book for the layer above the model: evaluation, prompt and context design, RAG, finetuning decisions, latency and cost. Paid.

📄 Papers & Primary References

  • Attention Is All You Need — Vaswani et al., NeurIPS (2017) Advanced

    The transformer. Every model on this page is a descendant, so it's worth reading even though it predates LLMs as a category.

  • Language Models are Few-Shot Learners — Brown et al., OpenAI / NeurIPS (2020) Advanced

    The GPT-3 paper, and the moment "just describe the task in the prompt" became a serious alternative to fine-tuning.

  • Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models — Kaplan et al., OpenAI (2020) Advanced

    Loss falls as a smooth power law in model size, data and compute — the empirical result that made spending billions look rational.

  • Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models — Hoffmann et al., DeepMind (2022) Advanced

    The "Chinchilla" correction: the previous generation was far too large for how little data it saw. Read it directly after Kaplan.

  • Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback — Ouyang et al., OpenAI (2022) Advanced

    InstructGPT — the RLHF recipe that turned a text predictor into an assistant. This is the paper that made ChatGPT possible.

  • Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model — Rafailov et al., NeurIPS (2023) Advanced

    Gets RLHF's result with a plain classification loss and no reward model or RL loop — now the default way most teams do alignment.

  • Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models — Wei et al., Google (2022) Intermediate

    Asking for intermediate steps unlocks arithmetic and multi-step reasoning the same model otherwise fails — the origin of everything "reasoning".

  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks — Lewis et al., NeurIPS (2020) Intermediate

    The paper that named RAG. Worth reading before you build one, because most production designs quietly diverge from it.

  • LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models — Hu et al., Microsoft (2021) Advanced

    Freeze the model, train two small matrices per layer. The reason fine-tuning a useful model no longer needs a datacentre.

  • The Llama 3 Herd of Models — Grattafiori et al., Meta (2024) Advanced

    Ninety-odd pages of what actually goes into a frontier model — data curation, infrastructure failures, post-training. Unusually candid.

  • DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning — DeepSeek-AI (2025) Advanced

    Reasoning behaviour emerging from reinforcement learning with almost no supervised examples — and an open recipe others could reproduce.

  • On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big? — Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major & Shmitchell, FAccT (2021) Intermediate

    The most influential critique of the scaling story — training-data bias, environmental cost, and fluency mistaken for understanding. Free PDF; also in the ACM proceedings.

✍️ Articles & Blog Posts

  • The Illustrated Transformer — Jay Alammar Beginner

    Still the clearest picture-by-picture walk through attention ever written. Read this before any paper on this page.

  • What Is ChatGPT Doing … and Why Does It Work? — Stephen Wolfram (2023) Beginner

    Book-length and free to read online, building up from "it just adds one word at a time" to embeddings and attention without assuming any background.

  • 2025: The year in LLMs — Simon Willison Beginner

    Twenty-six sections of what actually changed, by someone who tested it all. The fastest way to catch up on the current state of play.

  • Prompt Engineering — Lilian Weng, Lil'Log Intermediate

    Prompting treated as a research area rather than a list of tricks, with the papers behind each technique cited.

  • LLM Powered Autonomous Agents — Lilian Weng, Lil'Log Intermediate

    The reference decomposition of an agent into planning, memory and tool use — the vocabulary most later writing assumes.

  • Building Effective AI Agents — Anthropic (2024) Intermediate

    Argues most "agents" should be simple composable workflows instead, and names the five patterns worth knowing. Refreshingly anti-framework.

  • Understanding Reasoning LLMs — Sebastian Raschka (2025) Intermediate

    The four ways reasoning models get built — inference-time scaling, pure RL, SFT+RL, distillation — separated cleanly. Free to read.

  • Your AI Product Needs Evals — Hamel Husain Intermediate

    The corrective to endless prompt-fiddling: without a measurement loop you cannot tell whether a change helped. The single most useful post here if you're shipping.

🔧 Tools & Interactive

  • Tiktokenizer — live tokenizer playground

    Paste text and watch it split into tokens across different models. Five minutes here explains a lot of otherwise baffling model behaviour.

  • LLM Visualization — Brendan Bycroft

    A 3D walkthrough of a real GPT executing, one matrix multiply at a time. The best answer to "but what is it actually doing?"

  • nanochat — Andrej Karpathy

    The full pipeline — tokenize, pretrain, finetune, evaluate, serve — in minimal hackable code, for roughly $100 of GPU time.

  • Ollama — run open-weight models locally

    One command to get a model running on your own machine. Removes the API bill from experimentation, which changes how much you experiment.

  • llm (CLI) — Simon Willison

    Talk to any model from the terminal, pipe files in, log every response to SQLite. Ideal for building intuition through sheer volume of prompts.

  • LM Arena — blind head-to-head model comparison

    Vote on anonymised answers from two models, then see the leaderboard. The quickest calibration of which models are genuinely ahead.
