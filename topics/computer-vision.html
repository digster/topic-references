Computer Vision

Last updated: 2026-09-26

Computer vision is the problem of getting a computer to understand what a camera sees: which objects are in a picture, where they are, how far away they are and how they move. It is much harder than it looks. An image is only a grid of brightness values — a flat projection of a 3D world, in which the same object can look completely different under new lighting, from a new viewpoint, or half hidden behind something else. In 1966 an MIT memo proposed building "a significant part of a visual system" as a summer project; the field has been at it ever since.

Two traditions run through it. Geometry treats the camera as a measuring instrument: model how a lens projects the world, match the same points across several photos, and you can recover depth, camera motion and whole 3D scenes — the machinery behind panoramas, photogrammetry and the tracking in robots and headsets. Recognition is about naming what is in the picture, and it was rebuilt in 2012, when a convolutional network trained on ImageNet decisively beat pipelines built on hand-designed features such as SIFT and HOG. Vision transformers and large pretrained models — CLIP, DINOv2, Segment Anything — have since produced features that transfer to new tasks with little or no labelled data.

The two traditions are now meeting. A single network such as VGGT recovers cameras and depth from a set of photos in one forward pass, a job that used to need a hand-built geometry pipeline, and vision encoders have become the eyes of multimodal language models. This page follows that arc: image formation and filtering, features and geometry, deep recognition — classification, detection, segmentation — and finally 3D and the foundation models. Generating images is a topic of its own, on the sibling page linked at the bottom.

Good to know first: comfortable Python and NumPy — an image is just an array — plus linear algebra through matrix multiplication, with eigenvectors and the SVD for the geometry half, and calculus up to gradients. For the recognition half, know what a layer, a loss and backpropagation are; the deep learning page covers that, and its convolutional-network material is the direct on-ramp to this one.

📚 Books

  • The Worlds I See — Fei-Fei Li, Flatiron Books (2023) Beginner

    Paid. A memoir from the scientist who built ImageNet, and the clearest account of why a dataset — not a cleverer algorithm — turned out to be the missing piece. The human story behind 2012.

  • Foundations of Computer Vision — Antonio Torralba, Phillip Isola & William T. Freeman, MIT Press (2024) Beginner

    Free to read online. The newest textbook, written with learning built in from the start rather than bolted onto a classical course — imaging and filtering, neural networks and representation learning, geometry and generative models in one voice.

  • Modern Computer Vision with PyTorch (2nd ed.) — V Kishore Ayyadevara & Yeshwanth Reddy, Packt (2024) Intermediate

    Paid. The hands-on route: detection, segmentation, pose and OCR built in PyTorch, then CLIP, Segment Anything and diffusion. Every chapter's notebooks are on GitHub and open in Colab.

  • Computer Vision: Algorithms and Applications (2nd ed.) — Richard Szeliski, Springer (2022) Intermediate

    Free PDF for personal use from the author's site. The field's standard reference, from image formation through features, stereo and 3D reconstruction to deep recognition — best read by topic rather than cover to cover.

  • Multiple View Geometry in Computer Vision (2nd ed.) — Richard Hartley & Andrew Zisserman, Cambridge University Press (2004) Advanced

    Paid. The definitive text on the geometry half — projective geometry, camera calibration, epipolar geometry and bundle adjustment. The book's page has free sample chapters, including the one on epipolar geometry and the fundamental matrix.

📄 Papers & Primary References

  • The Summer Vision Project — Seymour Papert, MIT AI Memo AIM-100 (1966) Beginner

    A few pages proposing to build "a significant part of a visual system" over one summer with student workers. The field's most famous underestimate, and a good reminder of why seeing is hard.

  • Distinctive Image Features from Scale-Invariant Keypoints — David G. Lowe, International Journal of Computer Vision (2004) Intermediate

    SIFT: find keypoints that survive changes of scale and rotation, and describe each one well enough to match it in another photo. The backbone of panorama stitching and structure from motion for a decade. Free PDF from Lowe's page.

  • Random Sample Consensus — Martin A. Fischler & Robert C. Bolles, Communications of the ACM (1981) Intermediate

    RANSAC: fit a model to the smallest random sample of points, keep the fit most of the data agrees with, repeat. Still how nearly every geometry pipeline survives bad matches. Free from the ACM Digital Library.

  • Rapid Object Detection using a Boosted Cascade of Simple Features — Paul Viola & Michael Jones, CVPR (2001) Intermediate

    Integral images, boosted Haar-like features and an attentional cascade made face detection real-time on 2001 hardware — OpenCV's Haar cascades still implement it. Free copy hosted by CMU; the IEEE version is paywalled. Dalal & Triggs's HOG (2005) is the other classic hand-designed detector.

  • ImageNet: A Large-Scale Hierarchical Image Database — Deng, Dong, Socher, Li, Li & Fei-Fei, CVPR (2009) Beginner

    Millions of labelled images organised by WordNet — the dataset that made deep recognition possible. Its annual challenge is where AlexNet won in 2012; that paper is on the deep learning page.

  • U-Net: Convolutional Networks for Biomedical Image Segmentation — Olaf Ronneberger, Philipp Fischer & Thomas Brox, MICCAI (2015) Intermediate

    Segmentation as an encoder–decoder with skip connections, built for biomedical images with few labels. The same shape later became the backbone of diffusion image generators.

  • Faster R-CNN: Towards Real-Time Object Detection with Region Proposal Networks — Shaoqing Ren, Kaiming He, Ross Girshick & Jian Sun, NeurIPS (2015) Advanced

    The two-stage detector: a region proposal network shares features with the classifier, so finding and recognising objects become one network. The end of the line that began with R-CNN in 2014.

  • You Only Look Once: Unified, Real-Time Object Detection — Joseph Redmon, Santosh Divvala, Ross Girshick & Ali Farhadi, CVPR (2016) Intermediate

    Detection as a single regression over a grid: one pass, real time, less accurate at first. The ancestor of the YOLO family most practitioners actually deploy.

  • Mask R-CNN — Kaiming He, Georgia Gkioxari, Piotr Dollár & Ross Girshick, ICCV (2017) Advanced

    Adds a small mask branch to Faster R-CNN and gets instance segmentation — a separate outline for every object. ICCV 2017's best paper, and for years the standard baseline on COCO.

  • Structure-from-Motion Revisited — Johannes L. Schönberger & Jan-Michael Frahm, CVPR (2016) Advanced

    The paper behind COLMAP: incremental reconstruction of cameras and 3D points from unordered photos, plus the engineering that makes it robust. Free on CVF Open Access.

  • An Image is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale (ViT) — Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR (2021) Advanced

    Cut an image into patches, treat them as tokens, and a plain transformer matches convolutional networks once there is enough pretraining data. The encoder design most of the models below build on.

  • End-to-End Object Detection with Transformers (DETR) — Carion et al., ECCV (2020) Advanced

    Detection as set prediction: a transformer outputs a fixed set of boxes matched one-to-one against the ground truth, which removes anchor boxes and non-maximum suppression.

  • Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision (CLIP) — Radford et al., OpenAI / ICML (2021) Intermediate

    Trained on 400 million image–caption pairs to match pictures with text, so any list of class names becomes a zero-shot classifier. Its image encoder is the eye inside many vision-language models.

  • DINOv2: Learning Robust Visual Features without Supervision — Oquab et al., Meta AI / TMLR (2024) Advanced

    Self-supervised pretraining on curated images gives all-purpose features that work across tasks without fine-tuning — a common frozen backbone for depth, segmentation and retrieval. DINOv3 (2025) scales the recipe further and keeps its dense features from degrading in long training.

  • Segment Anything — Kirillov et al., Meta AI / ICCV (2023) Intermediate

    Click a point or draw a box and get a mask: a promptable segmenter trained on over a billion masks gathered by a model-in-the-loop data engine. SAM 2 (2024) extends it to video; SAM 3 (2025) takes phrases like "yellow school bus".

  • Visual Instruction Tuning (LLaVA) — Haotian Liu, Chunyuan Li, Qingyang Wu & Yong Jae Lee, NeurIPS (2023) Intermediate

    Connect a CLIP image encoder to a language model through a small projection and fine-tune on GPT-4-generated instructions — the simple recipe behind most open vision-language models.

  • VGGT: Visual Geometry Grounded Transformer — Wang, Chen, Karaev, Vedaldi, Rupprecht & Novotny, CVPR (2025) Advanced

    One feed-forward network predicts cameras, depth maps and point maps from one to hundreds of views in under a second, often beating pipelines that need a geometry optimisation step afterwards. CVPR 2025's best paper, building on DUSt3R.

✍️ Articles & Blog Posts

  • The state of Computer Vision and AI: we are really, really far away — Andrej Karpathy (2012) Beginner

    One photo — Obama with his foot on a colleague's scale — and everything you would need to understand to find it funny. The shortest argument that recognising objects is not the same as understanding a scene.

  • Understanding Convolutions — Christopher Olah (2014) Beginner

    Builds convolution from a ball dropped twice, then shows the same operation blurring an image and finding its edges — all before a neural network appears.

  • What I learned from competing against a ConvNet on ImageNet — Andrej Karpathy (2014) Beginner

    Karpathy labelled ImageNet test images himself: 5.1% top-5 error against GoogLeNet's 6.7%, a human baseline networks passed within months. Just as useful for what it shows about the dataset's quirks.

  • Feature Visualization — Chris Olah, Alexander Mordvintsev & Ludwig Schubert, Distill (2017) Intermediate

    Optimise an input image to excite one neuron and you see what it detects — edges, then textures, then object parts — layer by layer. Interactive, and the best look inside a trained vision network.

  • Object Detection for Dummies Part 3: R-CNN Family — Lilian Weng (2017) Intermediate

    R-CNN, Fast R-CNN, Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN in one pass, with the losses written out. Part 4 covers the one-stage detectors: YOLO, SSD and RetinaNet.

  • Vision Language Models Explained — Merve Noyan & Edward Beeching, Hugging Face (2024) Intermediate

    What a vision-language model is, how its image encoder and language model are joined and trained, and how to pick and fine-tune an open one. The 2025 follow-up covers what has changed since.

  • Contrastive Representation Learning — Lilian Weng (2021) Advanced

    The losses and tricks behind self-supervised vision — SimCLR, MoCo, BYOL, SwAV, Barlow Twins — and CLIP's image–text version of the same idea, in one long survey post.

    Free and short, in browser notebooks: build a Keras image classifier on a pretrained base, then take it apart — convolution, pooling, stride — and finish with data augmentation.

  • OpenCV-Python Tutorials — official OpenCV documentation Beginner

    The practical classical toolkit: filtering, edges, contours, feature detection and matching. The camera calibration chapter turns the geometry half into working code.

    Slides, notes and assignments for the course that taught a generation deep learning through vision. The 2025 lectures are free on YouTube.

    Self-contained notes on the geometry half — camera models, single-view metrology, epipolar geometry, stereo, fitting and matching. A gentler way in than Hartley & Zisserman.

  • TorchVision Object Detection Finetuning Tutorial — official PyTorch tutorials Intermediate

    Fine-tune a pretrained Mask R-CNN on a small pedestrian dataset — the quickest route from the detection papers above to a model that works on your own data.

🔧 Tools & Interactive

  • Image Kernels Explained Visually — Victor Powell, Setosa, in the browser

    Pick a kernel and watch it slide over a photo: blur, sharpen and edge detection happening pixel by pixel.

  • Teachable Machine — Google, in the browser

    Train an image classifier from your webcam in minutes with no code — a feel for what transfer learning does before you write it yourself.

  • OpenCV — open-source C++ and Python library

    The standard classical toolkit: filtering, features, calibration, stereo and a deep-learning inference module. scikit-image is the more Pythonic choice for image processing.

  • torchvision — PyTorch's vision library

    Datasets, transforms and pretrained classification, detection and segmentation models in one import. timm has the much larger zoo of pretrained backbones.

  • Ultralytics YOLO — Ultralytics, Python

    The quickest way to train and run a modern detector or segmenter on your own data. AGPL-3.0, so commercial use needs their enterprise licence.

  • Segment Anything Playground — Meta AI Demos, in the browser

    Try SAM 3 without installing anything: type a phrase or click an object and watch it segmented and tracked through a video.

  • COLMAP — Schönberger et al., open source

    Structure from motion and multi-view stereo from a folder of photos, with a GUI. Also the usual first step before training NeRF or Gaussian splatting.

🔗 Further Reading / Going Deeper

  • CVF Open Access — Computer Vision Foundation

    Every CVPR and ICCV paper since 2013, and WACV since 2020, free. Where the field actually publishes, and where to go once the classics above feel familiar.

  • Object Detection in 20 Years: A Survey — Zhengxia Zou, Keyan Chen, Zhenwei Shi, Yuhong Guo & Jieping Ye, Proceedings of the IEEE (2023)

    From Viola–Jones through the R-CNN and YOLO families to transformers, with the datasets and metrics each generation was judged on.

  • An Introduction to Vision-Language Modeling — Florian Bordes et al. (2024)

    A tutorial-length survey of how vision-language models are built, trained and evaluated, family by family — the next step after LLaVA and the Hugging Face explainer.

  • Do ImageNet Classifiers Generalize to ImageNet? — Benjamin Recht, Rebecca Roelofs, Ludwig Schmidt & Vaishaal Shankar, ICML (2019)

    Rebuild ImageNet's test set by the original recipe and accuracy drops 11–14 points — yet gains on the old test set still carry over. What a benchmark number does and doesn't tell you.

  • ImageNet-trained CNNs are biased towards texture — Robert Geirhos et al., ICLR (2019)

    Show a network a cat's outline filled with elephant skin and it answers "elephant". Humans go by shape, ImageNet CNNs by texture — and training on stylised images shifts them back.

  • Gender Shades: Intersectional Accuracy Disparities in Commercial Gender Classification — Joy Buolamwini & Timnit Gebru, FAT* / PMLR 81 (2018)

    Three commercial face-analysis systems misclassified darker-skinned women at error rates up to 34.7%, against at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men. Read it before trusting a single accuracy number for a face system.

  • Excavating AI: The Politics of Images in Machine Learning Training Sets — Kate Crawford & Trevor Paglen (2019)

    What the labels in ImageNet's person categories actually say, and what it means to classify people from photographs at all.
